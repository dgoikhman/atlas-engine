# Star Score Rankings, Q3 2026

**Florence, South Carolina leads BRRRR Market's Q3 2026 Star Score rankings at
87 out of 100, on an 8.6% gross rental yield and a typical home value of
$193,768 — one of only 12 metros in the 165-market index where a year of rent
comes to 8% or more of the purchase price.** The top five all pair a gross
yield of 7.9% or better with a price-to-rent ratio of 13 or lower, and all five
sit under a $215,000 typical home value. The index spans 58 points: Florence at
87 down to Redding, California at 29.

Ranks 2, 3 and 4 all score 86 and are separated by less than a point of

not a meaningful ranking signal.

**1. Florence, SC — 87/100, 8.6% yield.** Eight markets in the index max both
price-driven factors, scoring 100 on gross yield and 100 on entry price.
Florence leads because it is the one of those eight with no weak factor: its
lowest subscore is the 58 it takes on equity growth, where the other seven
bottom out anywhere from 48 down to 0. A $193,768 typical home value against
$1,395 rent produces a ratio of 12, South Carolina's 0.56% average effective
property-tax rate scores 91 on tax drag, and state landlord law scores 85. Its
60 on climate and insurance risk is the weakest of the top five on that
measure.

**2. Mobile, AL — 86/100, 8.1% yield.** Mobile maxes the two price-driven
factors — 100 on gross yield, 100 on entry price at a $197,652 typical value —
and adds a maxed tax score of 100, on Alabama's 0.40% average effective
property-tax rate. It also scores 90 on landlord-friendliness. What holds it at
86 is equity growth: a score of 38, meaning Mobile's five-year price
appreciation sits in the bottom four-tenths of the 242 metros in the dataset. This is a rent market, not an appreciation
market.

**3. Montgomery, AL — 86/100, 7.9% yield.** Montgomery is Mobile's profile with
growth and yield traded off against each other. It scores 56 on equity growth against
Mobile's 38 — an 18-point edge — and gives back ground on the price factors: 98
on gross yield and 89 on entry price at a $214,925 typical value, the most
expensive market in the top five. Same 90 on landlord law and same 100 on tax
drag as Mobile, both state-level inputs.

**4. Charleston, WV — 86/100, 10.1% yield.** Charleston has the highest gross
yield in the index and the only price-to-rent ratio below 11, at 10, on the
cheapest entry price of any ranked market: $150,171. It also carries the
strongest climate and insurance score in the top five, 80. It places fourth
rather than first for a mechanical reason worth understanding — see the note
below on where the yield factor stops counting — plus a 75 on
landlord-friendliness, the lowest of the five.

**5. Jackson, TN — 85/100, 8.2% yield.** Jackson clears 100 on gross yield at a
$207,877 typical value and $1,425 rent, and takes 90 on landlord law under
Tennessee statute. Its 94 on entry price and 86 on tax drag, against a 0.65%
average effective rate, are both a notch below the Alabama pair. Equity growth
of 46 is the constraint.

## The counterintuitive finding: Louisiana has the country's best yields and nothing in the top 25

**Shreveport and Monroe post 8.8% gross rental yields — the highest of any
ranked market except Charleston, West Virginia — and rank 29th and 32nd. No
Louisiana metro places higher than 29th, and the state puts seven markets in
the ranked index.** Shreveport scores a perfect 100 on gross yield and a
perfect 100 on entry price at a price-to-rent ratio of 11, and still finishes
with 72 points.

Two factors take it apart. Louisiana carries a climate and insurance risk score
of 25, the lowest state value anywhere in the index — below Florida's 40 and
Mississippi's 55 — reflecting hurricane exposure and the property-insurance
repricing that follows it. And Louisiana's five-year price appreciation is
close to the floor of the national cohort: Houma scores 0 on equity growth,
Lake Charles 1, New Orleans 2, Lafayette 3, Shreveport 7, Monroe 12. Those two
factors are 25% of the Star Score between them, and in Louisiana both are
close to empty. New Orleans is the clearest case: a 7.3% gross yield, a ratio
of 14, and 57 points for 82nd place.

The reading is not that Louisiana rents badly. It is that Louisiana's rent
yields are, in part, compensation for risks the index prices separately —
insurance cost and flat equity — and an investor underwriting a Shreveport
deal on the 8.8% headline alone is being paid for exactly the exposures that
number conceals.

One caveat on how to quote this: the climate and landlord factors are
state-level scores, so every Louisiana metro inherits the same 25 by
construction. The equity-growth figures are metro-level and measured from
Zillow's own price history.

### Where the yield factor stops counting

Charleston, West Virginia's 10.1% yield earns it exactly the same gross-yield
subscore as Mobile's 8.1%: 100. The factor maps 4.5% to 0 and 8.0% to 100 and
clamps there, so the 2.1 points of yield Charleston carries above 8.0% are
worth nothing to its score. Twelve of the 165 ranked markets sit at or above
8.0% and are therefore tied at 100 on the index's heaviest factor, and seven of
those twelve rank outside the top ten. Above 8% gross yield, the Star Score
stops rewarding cash flow and starts deciding on everything else — a design
choice, and a limit worth stating plainly in any citation of the top of this
table.

For the same reason the table's biggest gap is not at the top. Chicago posts a
7.5% gross yield at a ratio of 13 — numbers that would put it in the top
quartile on cash flow, and an 86 on the yield factor — and ranks 95th with 54
points, on 35 for landlord-friendliness, 7 for tax drag against Illinois's
2.08% average effective rate, and 0 on entry price, because a $360,262 typical
home value is above the $340,000 ceiling of the entry factor.

## Methodology

The Star Score is a 0-100 index of how well a US metro suits a buy-under-market,
renovate, rent, refinance and repeat strategy. It weights gross rental yield
35%, entry price against a $100,000 capital base 15%, landlord-friendliness
15%, equity growth 15%, property-tax drag 10%, and climate and insurance risk
10%. Each factor maps to a 0-100 subscore and the index is the weighted sum, as
implemented in `engine/scoring.py` and published on the site's `/methodology`
page.

The factor definitions: gross yield is annualized typical rent over typical
home value, mapped linearly from 4.5% (0) to 8.0% (100) and clamped at both
ends. Entry price maps a $340,000 typical home value to 0 and $200,000 or below
to 100. Equity growth is the metro's five-year change in Zillow Home Value
Index, expressed as a percentile within the 242-metro cohort. Property-tax drag
inverts the state average effective rate, mapping 2.2% to 0 and 0.4% or below
to 100. Landlord-friendliness and climate and insurance risk are editorial
0-100 scores set at state level on public data — statutes, eviction timelines
and disaster and insurance exposure — and they are judgments, not measurements.
Because they are state-level, metros in the same state share them.

Of the 242 metros in the dataset, 165 are ranked. The remaining 77 are carried
as reference markets, separated by a single rule: a price-to-rent ratio of 19 or
higher moves a metro out of the ranked table and into the reference list. They
are published to show the range of the index, not to recommend them. The
price-to-rent ratio is typical home value over twelve months of typical rent.

Yields and ratios in this report are gross. They are before vacancy,
maintenance, management, insurance and financing costs, all of which are local
and all of which matter more in the high-yield markets at the top of this table
than the headline suggests.

## Data vintage

Home values are Zillow Home Value Index (ZHVI) metro figures and rents are
Zillow Observed Rent Index (ZORI) metro figures, both **July 2026** — the most
recent vintage in the dataset behind this edition, refreshed 10 September 2026.
Equity growth is measured against July 2021 ZHVI. State property-tax rates are
Tax Foundation effective-rate tables; landlord and climate scores were last
reviewed September 2026. Supporting context comes from the US Census ACS, HUD
Fair Market Rents and state landlord-tenant statutes.

This is the first edition of these rankings built on the 165-market live
pipeline, so it reports no quarter-over-quarter movement. An earlier Q3 draft
covered an 18-metro seed dataset and is not comparable — rank and score changes
are not meaningful across that break. Movement will be reported from Q4 2026
forward, against this edition as the baseline.
